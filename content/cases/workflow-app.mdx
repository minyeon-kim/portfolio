## Context

Devsign is a workspace where designers and developers review the same change from their own side. It brings together a dashboard, a project workspace, docs, change history, team activity, and Merge Studio, a canvas for combining design variants.

The prototype runs on React with Tailwind CSS and shadcn/ui. A single token file, `design.md`, is the source of truth for color, spacing, radius, and control sizes across every page.

## Decisions

### Turn a mismatch into a decision, not a bug ticket

When a mismatch is detected, the card shows the related file and line, the cause, the impact, and a severity. The designer sees the original, the current implementation, and the developer's proposal side by side, then chooses one of three outcomes: keep the original, approve the change, or ask for another review. They write a reason and pick when it ships: now, before release, or in the next version.

### Keep design alternatives out of the review queue

Comparing variants, like four checkout options or two AI-generated dashboards, never adds an item to the change list or starts an approval. Only a real gap between the design and the code does.

### Tie approvals to the exact content reviewed

<Callout label="Security lens">

An approval in Devsign applies to the content as it was when review was requested. If the change is edited after the request, pending approvals are cancelled and the author has to request review again. It's the same rule as approving a specific commit rather than a branch: a sign-off can't silently carry over to something the reviewer never saw.

</Callout>

### Record every step as a History checkpoint

Requests, decisions, fixes, and resolutions each leave a checkpoint. Selecting an activity in History replays the change at that point and marks the related issue on the timeline.

## Outcome & learnings

We planned the usability test around three tasks in one project, with participants switching roles from the profile menu: merging two AI dashboard variants in Merge Studio as a designer, approving a design change as a designer, and resolving a detected mismatch as a developer. The plan observes whether people move from a review notification to the right change, whether History leads them to the matching replay, and whether they can tell a design alternative from a real mismatch.
