<TLDR>
- **Problem:** AI makes output faster. Comparing alternatives and agreeing on what ships still takes work across disconnected tools.
- **My role:** Product design in a two-person team, from research and workflow definition to a working prototype and iteration.
- **Outcome:** A functioning prototype refined through an initial usability test and a co-design session. The revised experience has not yet been retested.
</TLDR>

<CaseSection id="overview" label="01 / Overview" title="From AI output to a shared decision.">

Devsign brings designers and developers into one workflow to compare AI-generated ideas, combine the parts that work, and resolve differences between design and implementation.

The project connects the steps that usually happen across screenshots, messages, design comments, and code: discover a change, request a decision, agree on it, and follow it through.

</CaseSection>

<CaseSection id="challenge" label="02 / The challenge" title="Making became faster. Agreement did not.">

AI tools generate screens and code quickly, but teams still need to compare the results, choose a direction, and explain why something changed.

When implementation diverges from the design, the reason, owner, and approval can end up in different places. Our focus was the work after generation: comparing, deciding, and keeping that decision in context.

</CaseSection>

<CaseSection id="research" label="03 / Discover & define" title="Understand the handoffs, not just the tools.">

We began with community content and competing services, then interviewed four practitioners across design, product management, and development to understand how AI output moves between roles.

We grouped interview observations into themes including fragmented tools, shared context, manual review, ownership, and version management. The recurring need was to make decisions easier to find and act on—not to hand every judgment to AI.

</CaseSection>

<CaseSection id="merge-studio" label="05 / Merge Studio" title="Choose the best parts. Build one direction.">

Instead of picking an entire AI-generated variation, designers can compare alternatives side by side and combine selected regions—for example, the card layout from one idea and the buttons from another.

The combined result can be edited directly, while the origin of each selected part remains visible.

<CaseSection id="change-review" label="06 / Change review" title="A difference becomes a decision.">

When implementation differs from the design, developers can share the reason and a proposed change. Designers compare the original, the implementation, and the proposal before keeping the original, approving the change, or requesting another review.

Approval is one step in the flow. The developer still needs to make and verify the change before it is resolved.

</CaseSection>

<CaseSection id="workspace-history" label="07 / Workspace & history" title="Keep the screen and its story together.">

The Screens board lays out a project's views in flow order, with markers that lead to the relevant changes. Teams can first see where something changed, then inspect the details.

<CaseSection id="testing" label="08 / Test & iterate" title="Both roles saw it as someone else's tool.">

In the first usability test, one designer and one developer worked through the prototype. Both needed help understanding what to do next, especially during the request and approval flow.

The experience could be completed, but the first-use hierarchy and ownership were unclear. We used those observations to separate role-specific scenarios and make the next action more visible.

</CaseSection>

<CaseStatement>“What should I do now?” became the question we designed around.</CaseStatement>

<CaseSection id="codesign" label="09 / Co-design" title="See it. Understand it. Decide.">

A co-design session with a practicing designer helped us simplify the workspace, bring the request and its reason to the top, and make decision controls easier to find.

We moved toward a screen-led experience, clearer terminology, and a step-by-step review flow. Merge Studio became part of the same workspace so the surrounding context stayed in place.

</CaseSection>

<CaseSection id="reflection" label="10 / Reflection & next steps" title="A clearer workflow. Still more to validate.">

The project reframed design–development collaboration around roles and decisions, rather than a collection of features. A working prototype made it possible to observe actual clicks and revise the flow with participants.

The revised design has not had a second usability test. The next step is to retest it with new scenarios and validate the workflow in a real team, including Figma and Git integrations and organization-specific approval policies.

</CaseSection>
